Not every server deserves the same terminal.

Give each host a label: Personal, Staging, Production, or one of your own. Clavra takes it from there: calm where it can be, careful where it has to be.

  • Personal

    Your VPS, the Pi in the cupboard. Nothing in your way: just the terminal, fast.

  • Staging

    Where you try things. Clavra remembers what you ran and offers it back as you type.

  • Production

    A coral mark on every pane, and a yes before anything you can't undo. Nothing it remembers is kept on disk.

A yes before rm -rf.

Press Return on rm -rf, DROP TABLE, a DELETE without WHERE, shutdown, docker compose down or a force push on a production host, and Clavra holds it back until you confirm. Pastes, snippets and every split pane are checked too.

Split it, and type less.

⌘D splits beside, ⇧⌘D splits below, the way you know from iTerm. Commands you ran on that host before appear in grey after the cursor; → takes them. Find in the scrollback, type into every pane at once, and when Wi-Fi drops, the session comes back on its own with its tunnels.

Files, beside the terminal.

Browse the server over SFTP without leaving the tab. Drop files or whole folders to upload, download folders with everything in them, and open any file in your Mac editor: every save goes back up. Uploads land under a temporary name first, so a dropped connection never leaves half a file.

Tunnels in one click.

Clavra lists the ports the server listens on, PostgreSQL, Redis, your app, and forwards one with a click. Local, remote and SOCKS tunnels, saved with the host so they open on every connect, and jump hosts for servers behind a bastion.

Made for the bad day.

The moments an SSH client usually leaves you on your own.

  • What it would touch

    Before a held command runs, a look-alike that only reads checks the server: 1,243 files, 3.2 GB; the units that depend on the service; the containers compose down takes with it.

  • Undo for rm -rf

    Back Up, Then Run archives what rm or a recursive chmod would touch, on the server, first. Changed your mind? Put it all back from the status line.

  • Recordings, as video

    Press Record in a tab and Stop when you're done. Every command is a marker on the timeline, passwords and tokens are hidden, and any recording exports as an MP4.

  • Sessions that stay

    Each tab can run inside tmux on the server, set up for you. Wi-Fi drops, sleep or quitting Clavra don't stop what's running; reconnecting lands you where you were.

  • sudo with Touch ID

    When sudo asks, Clavra fills the password from the Keychain after Touch ID. It's never typed, so it never lands in the scrollback or the history.

  • Down, and back

    Production hosts are checked every minute. Two misses in a row and you get a notification, and another when it answers again. Quiet while your own Mac is offline.

  • Compare two servers

    Side by side: packages, running services, listening ports, a config file, or a whole folder by checksum. Find what's different between staging and production.

  • Logs and runbooks

    Follow a log, the journal or a container beside the terminal, errors standing out. Keep a Markdown runbook per host whose commands run with a click.

Your tabs, on your phone.

Away from the desk and the worker needs a restart? Turn on Phone Access in Clavra, scan the code with your phone's camera, and allow it on the Mac. Your tabs open in the phone's browser, with Esc, Tab, Ctrl and the arrows a tap away. There's nothing to install.

And the parts you'd expect to be right.

Keys, checked
Every key in ~/.ssh with its type, age and the hosts that use it. Weak or world-readable keys are called out; a new Ed25519 key is one click. Ed25519, RSA and ECDSA all sign in.
Host keys, verified
The first connection shows the fingerprint to trust. A changed key gets a red warning, checked against your ~/.ssh/known_hosts too.
Server health
How fast each host answers, in the sidebar. While connected: load, memory, disk and uptime, and anything running hot turns coral.
Snippets
The commands you keep reaching for, typed into any tab with ⌘;. Risky ones still ask on production.
Your ~/.ssh/config
See every host before it's imported, with Host * defaults, Include and jump hosts followed. Keys are matched to hosts for you.
Secrets in the Keychain
Passwords and passphrases never touch a file. No account, no analytics, no telemetry.
Updates in place
Once a day Clavra asks GitHub for a newer version, checks it's signed by us, and restarts into it. You can turn the check off.
On every Mac you use
Keep hosts and snippets in your own iCloud Drive if you like. Each Mac keeps its own passwords.

Questions

Is Clavra free?

Yes. Clavra is free to download and use, with no account and no subscription. The source is on GitHub.

Which commands does Clavra hold back on production?

Commands that are hard to take back: rm -rf, DROP TABLE or DATABASE, DELETE or UPDATE without WHERE, shutdown and reboot, docker rm, docker compose down, kubectl delete, terraform destroy, git push --force, chmod -R, firewall flushes and more. Nothing reaches the server until you confirm. You can turn the guard on for any host, not only production.

Where are my passwords and keys stored?

Passwords and key passphrases are kept in your Mac's Keychain. Keys stay in ~/.ssh. Your hosts are a file on your Mac, or in your own iCloud Drive if you turn sync on. Clavra has no servers that hold your data.

Can clavra.naqara.dev read my terminal when I use Phone Access?

No. The phone and the Mac encrypt everything with ChaCha20-Poly1305 under a key that exists only in the QR code. The relay passes along data it cannot read, and every new phone has to be allowed on the Mac. If you'd rather not use the relay at all, Phone Access can also work directly over your Wi-Fi.

Does Clavra work with my ~/.ssh/config?

Yes. Clavra shows every host in ~/.ssh/config before importing it, with its user, port, key and jump host, following Host * defaults and Include. Entries that only pick a key for git are left out. Keys are matched to hosts automatically, and host keys already in ~/.ssh/known_hosts are trusted.

Why does macOS ask me to confirm the first launch?

Clavra isn't notarized by Apple yet. Open it once, then go to System Settings, Privacy & Security, and click Open Anyway. That's needed once; updates install from inside the app after that.

Which Macs does Clavra run on?

Any Mac with macOS 15 or later, Apple Silicon or Intel.
